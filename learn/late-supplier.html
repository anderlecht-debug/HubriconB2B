Lesson 1 of 6 · 5 min

The date on your PO is a hope

Your supplier gives you a ready date, and you plan your stock around it. Here is how often that date holds at a typical busy supplier.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

A supplier is 85% booked and promises your order in 20 days. Out of 10 orders, how many are ready by that date?

About 6 in 10. In a simulated year at one typical supplier this busy, 62% of its 293 orders were ready by the promised date. The rest ran late: the worst 1 in 10 by 12 days or more, and the latest by 25 days.

A promised date isn't a lie. It's how long your order takes if nothing is ahead of it in line. Making it takes about 12 of the 20 days. The rest is the supplier's guess about the line of other brands' orders, and they can't see next month's orders any better than you can.

So most orders come in on time, and you start to trust the date. Then one doesn't, and that's the one that empties your shelf.

Remember

Plan on the date 9 orders in 10 make, not the date on the PO.

Do it on yours · 10 minutes

Pull your last 10 purchase orders. For each one, write three dates: the day you placed it, the ready date your supplier promised, and the day it was actually ready. Lesson 5 turns them into your supplier's score.

Lesson 2 of 6 · 6 min

Busy suppliers are late suppliers

Your supplier makes product for many brands besides you. When their orders pile up, yours waits in line.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

Your supplier goes from half booked to 90% booked. How much longer does a new order wait in line before work starts on it?

About 9 times as long. A line grows much faster than the work feeding it. At half booked, a new order rarely finds anything ahead of it. At 90%, almost every order does, and the line takes weeks to clear even after things calm down. That's the textbook queue: the wait grows with busy ÷ (1 − busy), so 0.5 ÷ 0.5 = 1, and 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = 9.

Drag the slider and watch the typical supplier. Below about 70% busy, lateness barely moves. Above it, it climbs fast. At 50% busy, the typical simulated supplier's worst 1 in 10 orders is 3 days late. At 90%, it's 24 days, and only 35% of orders are on time.

That's why lateness comes in waves. Your supplier isn't getting worse at their job. Every brand they make for is ordering at once, and you can't see any of those orders.

Remember

When your supplier gets busy, every date slips, and the busier they are, the faster it slips.

Do it on yours · 2 minutes a month

Ask your supplier the same question on the first of every month: "If I placed an order today, when would it be ready?" Write the answer down. When that date starts moving out, they're filling up, and your next order should go out sooner.

Lesson 3 of 6 · 5 min

The weeks you can see coming

Some lateness is a surprise. Some of it is on the calendar every year.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

Orders placed in which month are most likely to run late?

February. Factories across China close for Lunar New Year, most for two to three weeks, and every brand's orders stack up behind the restart. Across the simulated suppliers, the worst 1 in 10 orders placed in February was ready 35 days after its promised date. In August, 18 days.

The real holiday moves every year, between late January and mid February, and the slowdown runs past it: workers travel home, and not all of them come back on day one. The second wave comes in autumn, when every brand orders for the holidays at the same factories.

Both are on the calendar a year ahead, so neither should surprise you.

Remember

Order before the line forms, not after. Lunar New Year and holiday ordering are on the calendar every year.

Do it on yours · 5 minutes

Put next Lunar New Year in your calendar (in 2027 it falls on February 6). From that day, count back your supplier's promised lead time plus a week, because many factories wind down early. That's the last day to place an order you need before March. Set a reminder two weeks before it.

Lesson 4 of 6 · 6 min

What a late order costs you

You pay for lateness one of two ways: your stock runs out, or you hold extra just in case. Here's what each costs, on your numbers.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

You sell $50,000 a month from one supplier. Which costs more: running out for 14 days once, or holding 14 extra days of stock for a whole year?

Running out, by about 5 times. A stockout loses the profit on every sale you can't make: $12,833 here. Extra stock costs only what it takes to hold it for a year, $2,625 at a 25% yearly holding cost. The catch is the cash: that cover ties up $10,500, which is why most brands hold too little.

This leaves out what a stockout does after it ends. A listing that runs dry tends to lose its rank, and some customers buy elsewhere and don't come back. So the real gap is wider than this.

The answer isn't a blanket month of extra stock either. It's the right amount for how late your supplier really runs, and less when you can see a delay coming. The next two lessons get you that number.

Remember

Running out costs you margin. Holding extra costs you carrying. Size the extra to how late your supplier really runs.

Do it on yours · 3 minutes

Put in your top product's numbers for the supplier that worries you most. Write down the first figure. That's what one late order can cost you, and what the rest of this course is worth.

Lesson 5 of 6 · 7 min

Score your own supplier

Three dates per order are all it takes: when you ordered, when the supplier promised it, and when it was really ready.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

Which one number should you track for each supplier?

How late the worst 1 in 10 runs. The average hides the bad orders, and those are the ones that empty your shelf. The on-time share counts a one-day slip the same as a 30-day one. The latest order ever is a single story. The 1-in-10 figure is the lateness nine orders in ten come in under, and it's what your extra stock has to cover.

Read the scores this way. The on-time share tells you how often the date holds. The 1-in-10 figure tells you how much cover you need. If one supplier's 1-in-10 is twice another's, it needs twice the cover, or twice the lead time, for the same product.

With fewer than 10 orders, the 1-in-10 figure is mostly your single worst order. Treat it as a starting point, and let every new order sharpen it.

Remember

Track one number per supplier: how late the worst 1 in 10 orders runs.

Lesson 6 of 6 · 6 min

Order on the real date

You know how late your supplier runs. Now turn it into the day your next order goes out.

VideoThis lesson, taught on screen on its own charts, will play here.

Guess first

Your supplier's worst 1 in 10 orders runs 12 days late. What covers it?

Either one, because it's the same thing. Ordering 12 days earlier means 12 more days of stock on hand when the order lands. That is the extra stock: the same units and the same cash. What a firm date gets you is a promise, and lesson 1 showed what those are worth. What changes the bill is knowing when you need the cushion: a quiet supplier needs less of it, and a slammed one needs more.

What one brand can't see

Everything so far uses your own past orders. They tell you how late a supplier has run, not how busy it is today. The supplier's other customers can tell you that: when several brands that share a supplier pool their order dates, the line shows up in the data, weeks before it reaches your order.

In Hubricon's simulation of 60 suppliers, five brands pooling their dates cut the average forecast miss from 5.7 days to 4.5. That's a simulation. Real orders are scored the same way, one report at a time, and none is published yet.

Remember

Order when stock falls to a day's sales × (promised days + the 1-in-10 days late + shipping). Check it again whenever your supplier gets busy.

Do it on yours · 10 minutes

Set the reorder point for your top product on the worksheet's Reorder sheet, using the 1-in-10 figure from lesson 5. Put a reminder in your calendar on the day it says.

Six rules for a supplier you can't see inside

  1. A promised date is the best case. Plan on the date 9 orders in 10 make.
  2. Busy suppliers are late suppliers. Ask every month when a new order would be ready, and order sooner when that date moves out.
  3. The calendar is no surprise. Order before Lunar New Year and the autumn rush, not after.
  4. A stockout costs margin; extra stock costs carrying. Size the extra to how late your supplier really runs.
  5. One number per supplier. How late the worst 1 in 10 orders runs.
  6. Order on the real date. Reorder at a day's sales × (promised + 1-in-10 late + shipping).
