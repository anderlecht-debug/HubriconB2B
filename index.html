  1. 01

    “Sales are up. The payout isn't.”

    Fees, landed cost and ad spend live in three different reports. No single number tells you what a SKU actually keeps.

    MARGIN.DECOMPWhat each SKU actually keeps
    NetSKU = Revenue − Σ |platform fees| − Units × clanded − Ads × RevSKU / Revtotal

    clanded = unit cost + inbound freight + packaging + other. Fees summed across every fee column the platform's own economics report carries — Amazon's referral, FBA and storage fees, or Shopify's payment processing, shipping labels and apps; ad spend allocated by revenue share within the period. The fees are also split: f = the share that is a percentage of the sale (referral), F = the part charged per unit whatever the price (FBA fulfilment, storage spread over the period's units). That split is what formula 03 needs, and where an export reports one blended fee line instead, the report says so rather than inventing the split.

    What it hands you

    Every SKU's true net margin — and the SKUs that go negative once ads are honestly allocated. Those get fixed or cut first.

  2. 02

    “I raised prices and made less.”

    The price moved without anyone knowing how demand responds. Two-thirds of sellers raised in 2024; most of them lost money doing it.

    ELASTICITY.FITHow your demand answers a price
    ln Q = α + ε · ln P (+ γ · ln Sessions) ε ± t0.975, dof · SEHC3 ε̂ = w · ε + (1 − w) · ε̄ w = τ² / (τ² + SE²)

    Log-log OLS per SKU. Guardrails run first: at least 5 periods and a price that actually moved (CV ≥ 2%) — otherwise the answer is “not enough data”, never a number that looks like a finding. Three things then keep the interval honest. The critical value is Student-t on the fit's own degrees of freedom, not 1.96: at five periods it is 3.18, and 1.96 would print a range 38% narrower than the data supports. The standard error is HC3 heteroskedasticity-consistent, because log-demand noise is not constant across a SKU's price range. And each SKU's ε is shrunk toward your catalog's by w — the ratio of between-SKU spread τ² to that SKU's own sampling variance — so a thin SKU borrows strength and a well-measured one keeps its own answer. You see both numbers and the weight.

    What it hands you

    ε per SKU with the honest range around it, and how much of it is your own SKU's history versus your catalog's. Below −1, a cut grows profit. Between −1 and 0, a careful increase converts almost straight into margin. On simulated sellers, 95% of these ranges contain the truth 94–97% of the time.

  3. 03

    “Fine — what price, exactly?”

    Elasticity gives you the direction. Profit calculus gives you the destination, to the cent.

    PRICE.OPTIMUMPrice-testing program
    Π(P) = Q₀ · (P / P₀)ε × (P · (1 − f) − c − F) dΠ/dP = 0 ⇒ P* = [(c + F) / (1 − f)] · ε / (1 + ε) step: max E[ΔΠ] − Var[ΔΠ] / 2B s.t. ES₅[ΔΠ] ≥ −B, |ΔP/P| ≤ 5%

    Valid for elastic SKUs (ε < −1). f = the fee rate that scales with price, F = the fee charged per unit whatever the price, c = landed unit cost. Treating F as if it scaled with the price — which is what one blended fee rate does — puts P* more than 5% below the truth on a typical FBA SKU. How far we walk is solved for, not set. The step maximises the expected gain less the variance it carries, priced at a risk budget B of 15% of that SKU's own monthly profit, subject to the worst-5% outcome staying inside B. Double the uncertainty on ε and the step shrinks; widen it far enough and the engine declines to move at all. ±5% a cycle is the outer rail your terms authorise, not the answer. Buy Box share (Amazon) or conversion rate (Shopify) is watched while the step is live.

    What it hands you

    An exact new price per SKU, the expected profit delta per period, its 5th-to-95th-percentile range, and how often the move goes the other way — then the next step once the result is measured. Where ε cannot be separated from −1, P* is unbounded and no destination is quoted: you get a direction, a small step and a plain sentence saying why, never a price to the cent the data cannot support.

  4. 04

    “I can't tell where my ad spend stops paying.”

    Your PPC tool or your Meta ads manager optimizes toward the ACoS or ROAS you typed in. Nobody derived that number from your margin.

    SPEND.RESPONSEWhere your ad dollar stops paying
    Sales(s) = a · sh / (kh + sh) Break-even: dSales/ds = 1 / m Bleed = terms: spend > $10, sales = 0

    Hill saturation fit per campaign, log-curve fallback. m = contribution margin before ads, so ads break even on profit, not on revenue. The 20–40% of spend leaking to search terms or ad sets that never convert is listed by term, in dollars — Sponsored Products, Google, or Meta.

    What it hands you

    The spend level where the next dollar returns less than it costs, and every leaking search term — negative-matched on the first cycle.

  5. 05

    “I stock out. Then I overstock.”

    A reorder sized on average velocity ignores that lead time and demand are both random. One of them always surprises you.

    MONTE_CARLO.RUNYour odds of running out
    L ~ LogNormal(lead time, σ = 0.2) ri = μi · exp(σi Zi − σi²/2) Zi = ρ Zcommon + √(1−ρ²) εi D ~ Poisson(r · L) P(stockout) = P(D > on hand + inbound) ROP = μd μL + 1.645 · √(μL σd² + μd² σL²)

    Twenty thousand simulated lead-time demand paths per SKU. The closed-form reorder point (95% service, Z = 1.645) runs alongside as a cross-check — the two must agree. Demand rates are lognormal, moment-matched to what your exports showed, and they share a common factor ρ estimated from your own catalog: a bad month is bad across your SKUs, not independently per SKU. That changes nothing about one SKU's odds and everything about how many run out at once — on a 24-SKU test catalog, the 95th percentile of simultaneous stockouts goes from 10 under independence to 15 with the shared factor. Both numbers are reported.

    What it hands you

    A stockout probability per SKU, a reorder date and a quantity — from the whole distribution of outcomes, not one average.

  6. 06

    “How much safety stock is actually worth carrying?”

    A flat 95% service target treats a 40%-margin SKU and a 6%-margin SKU the same. The money doesn't.

    NEWSVENDORInventory economics · fee cliffs
    q* = Cu / (Cu + Co) Cu = unit margin + low-inventory fee Co = storage + capital + obsolescence Order-up-to = q*-quantile of D(L+7d)

    The critical fractile: the in-stock probability the money justifies, SKU by SKU. Capital at 12%/yr, obsolescence 2% of cost per cycle, Amazon's aged-inventory and peak-season storage cliffs priced in; on Shopify, your 3PL's rate or nothing at all.

    What it hands you

    The service level each SKU earns — 99% for a fat margin, 85% for a thin one — the order quantity, the wire amount, and a hold-vs-liquidate call on excess.

  8. 08

    “Fees crept up and I noticed a quarter late.”

    A $0.30-per-unit fee change or a conversion drop is invisible inside a monthly total.

    ANOMALY.SCANChange detection · every cycle
    z = (xlatest − median) / (1.4826 MAD) CUSUM: S⁺ = max(0, S⁺ + z − k) k = 0.5σ, alarm at h = 4σ q = BH(p₁…pm) over the sweep finding ⟺ q ≤ 0.05

    Robust z on every fee, conversion and spend series; Page's CUSUM finds the period a level shift began. Then the part that matters on a real catalog: those are thousands of tests a sweep, and a threshold set for one series fires on noise many times over when you run it thousands of times. Every test carries a p-value against its own simulated null, Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false-discovery rate across the whole sweep, and nothing is a finding unless its q-value clears 5%. On 500 pure-noise SKUs the detectors alone call 548 of 4,000 tests a finding; none of them survives. You are told how many tests stood behind the alerts you did get.
