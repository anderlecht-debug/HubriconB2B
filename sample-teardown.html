Part three: reproduce it

Synthetic catalogue, real engine, not a client result. Tarnhollow is a fictional kitchen-storage brand made up for this page. Every row of data the engine read, every dollar figure below and every planted answer was invented. None of it is a client, a client result or any real seller's data, and no figure here is a forecast for any business.

Why invented. Hubricon has no clients yet, so there is no real Teardown to show, and a real seller's numbers would not be ours to publish. A catalogue whose answers were fixed in advance is the one place the engine's work can be checked against the truth.

What is real. The Hubricon engine at git b3b2bd6, with no engine source edited for this run — though at run time its working tree carried someone else's uncommitted changes in 7 files, none of them a model, a parser, the move drafting or the report, and a clean checkout of b3b2bd6 has not been tested (Reproduce it). It read 34 Amazon-shaped exports through its own parsers: 24 SKUs, 12 monthly periods, about $3.08M of sales a year. It ran all 11 of its models, with 20,000 simulated inventory paths per SKU at seed 42, then drafted its moves. Offline: an in-memory database, local files and a pinned date stood in for production, as Reproduce it sets out.

One catalogue, one seed. The page shows how the engine behaves and where it breaks. It is not a hit rate. The planted demand follows the curve shapes the engine assumes, which flatters it (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 9); in other ways, listed under Reproduce it, the planted world is harder than the engine assumes.

Names. Amazon is named only because the files copy the layout of its reports; nothing here is connected to or endorsed by Amazon. No brand called Tarnhollow turned up in a web search; that is not a trademark search, and if the name is yours we will change it.

Corrections. If anything here is wrong, write to hagen.simmons@hubricon.com. We correct it on this page and note the correction at its foot.

What we cite. MATH_METHODS.md is the engine's internal methods note, cited ten times below for the limits it records. It is not published on this site. Ask and the cited sections are sent in full, or read out on a call. The generator, the run script and the answer key are not published either — see Reproduce it.

Part one

The Teardown, as the engine wrote it

What a client's Teardown is built from, in the sections of the engine's report, run here on the invented one. What lands in a client's inbox differs from this page in three ways, and this page is the cleaner one. The covering letter is drafted by a language model from a table of figures the engine renders — the model is barred in code from writing any number, and a draft containing one is rejected (narrate.py) — the recorded walkthrough uses a synthetic voice, and today both go out without the founder reading them first. The engine's words are paraphrased except where quoted. Where the report template and the drafted moves disagree (defect E4 in part two), this page shows the drafted moves.

The moves

The engine drafted 37 moves and ranked them. 26 fall under a standing mandate: price steps inside the 5% cap, negative keywords, the branded-term pause and campaign trims. The other 11 wait for a yes: reorders, the claims filing, the peak storage, liquidation and low-inventory-fee calls, the loss-making SKU and the fee check.

12 moves carry an expected dollar figure. The other 25 do not, on purpose: no dollars are promised for the 5 reorders (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 5), and 20 of the 22 price steps carry no figure because the fit cannot support one. Those 20 are listed under Pricing.

The dollar figures run on different clocks, shown in the Clock column, so this page never adds them into one total.

How to read this

Standing means the client authorised that whole module at kickoff. In the code (issue.py) only the standing flag is read: a narrower step limit a client asks for is stored on their mandate and never compared against the drafted step, so honouring a narrowed limit is the founder's own check today, not something the code enforces. A standing move is emailed first and waits 72 hours (VETO_HOURS = 72), and that window cannot open if the email did not go out. What the code does when the window closes is approve. It does not read replies: a “no” reaches the record through the portal's Decline button, or by the founder typing it in before the window closes. An explicit move waits for a written yes. At most 5 moves are issued per weekly sweep, so a list this long would reach a client in batches. Expected dollars are written down before a move goes live. What is measured afterwards is narrower than that sounds: the 14-day after-window is applied to ad-bleed terms, spend steps and branded pauses, while a fee correction or a campaign trim can be graded at $0 early and never re-graded, and the “still present in the latest export” guard is a line of documentation rather than code. Nothing on this page was executed, so nothing on it was measured.

How a step without a dollar figure reads, word for word (rank 18):

Raise TH-BAKE-MAT-3 5.0% as a measured step: $17.20 → $18.06. We cannot separate this SKU's demand from the break-even point where a price move pays for itself (ε = -1.23, and the history supports anywhere from -2.85 to 0.39), and the optimum is unbounded at that point — so there is no destination to quote and no dollar figure we would stand behind. The step is sized to what the range allows; the next two periods narrow it. Buy Box watched while the step is live.

One phrase in that text claims more than the engine can deliver. “The next two periods narrow it” does not hold on its own: the step is chosen by the same fit it would be testing, and a two-week step barely shows in monthly data, so the engine's own steps do not pin down price sensitivity (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 1). What does hold is a small step and no dollar figure. The Buy Box line does not hold as written either — see the note under Pricing: watching a live step is a founder routine, not code.

Inventory: stockout odds and reorders

Synthetic catalogue, real engine, not a client result

20,000 simulated lead times per SKU. 5 SKUs sit at or above the 25% line, meaning that share of simulations or more ran out before a reorder placed today could land. The engine drafted a reorder for each, sized to the service level that SKU's margin justifies. The table shows the 9 SKUs at 10% or higher; the other 15 are each under 5%.

Demand across the catalogue moves together: the average pairwise correlation of log demand across the catalogue is 0.39, measured from 12 shared months and then pulled a quarter of the way toward the engine's 0.25 default, because twelve periods is a thin panel (dependence.py: weight = periods ÷ (periods + 4)). The run recorded no interval around it, so how firm that 0.39 is cannot be read off this page. Taking it at 0.39, the engine expects 4.7 SKUs to run out across their lead times, and 8 at the 95th percentile — against 7 if the same 24 SKUs were drawn independently, which is the cost of the correlation.

Fee lines, estimated from the engine's fee schedule and not taken from an Amazon bill: the low-inventory-level fee on 3 SKUs, $1,193 a month; the Q4 peak storage premium, $4,310 a month on stock still on hand in October, if it could be moved at no cost; and 850 units of TH-UTENSIL-CROCK aged 181 days or more, with a $353 monthly surcharge. The engine's call on that SKU: liquidate 907 units.

How to read this

Stockout odds are the share of simulated lead times in which stock runs out before a reorder placed today arrives. No dollars are promised for a reorder, because a stockout that did not happen cannot be measured (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 5). Order quantities come from the newsvendor rule: the service level is set by what a lost sale costs against what a leftover unit costs, storage and the season included. Two known gaps: the demand rate carries no seasonality, and lead-time spread is assumed at 0.2.

Pricing: elasticity intervals and refusals

Elasticity is fitted for each SKU from 12 months of prices and units, then pulled toward the catalogue's pooled −2.12 in proportion to how little the SKU's own history pins down (its own weight, below). Then the engine decides what the fit can support:

  • 2 SKUs get a destination price and a dollar figure, because the fit separates them from −1.
  • 20 SKUs get a small step and no dollar figure, because the fit cannot separate them from −1 and the best price cannot be named.
  • 2 SKUs get no price instruction. TH-FOOD-CONT-5: price barely moved all year (coefficient of variation under 2%): no elasticity is fitted, so no price is named. TH-BAG-CLIP-20: the risk-averse step search could not beat standing still, or wanted a step under 0.5%.

How to read this

Elasticity (ε) is the percent change in units sold for a 1% change in price. Below −1, demand is price-sensitive and a cut can pay. Between −1 and 0, a careful rise mostly adds margin. The 5% cap is in code (pricing_engine, STEP_CAP = 0.05). Two things beside it are not: nothing in the execute command opens a price test, and the daily Buy Box reading is typed in by hand, so watching a live step is the founder's own routine today rather than an automatic check. Holding a SKU to one step per cycle is held by hand too; there is no cooldown in code. The fit reads prices the seller chose, often in response to demand; that bias is measured but not corrected (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 1).

Ads: zero-sale terms and break-even

Synthetic catalogue, real engine, not a client result

21 search-term rows spent more than $10 with zero attributed sales across the July and August exports: $3,477.88 over 13 terms in 4 campaigns. Rows at $10 or less are left out by design.

Each campaign's spend-response curve is fitted over 92 days of spend. Break-even is the daily spend at which the last dollar of ads brings back $3.19 of sales, which at this catalogue's margin before ads — 31.4% to one decimal place — is where it stops paying for itself. Both figures come from the run's unrounded margin, so 1 ÷ 0.314 gives $3.18 rather than reproducing $3.19 exactly.

Branded search: $1,375.78 went to 6 search terms containing the brand's name in August. The pause move rests on an assumed share, not a measurement (see the note under The moves).

How to read this

Zero-sale terms are listed in dollars, to be negative-matched. The saving assumes the negatives cost no sales. Trims are meant to be sized to the cautious end of the fitted break-even interval, the end that promises less; on SP - Storage - Broad that end is the estimate itself, so for that campaign the phrase means nothing (E3). Break-even is not solved for: the engine walks a 400-point grid running from three times the campaign's highest observed daily spend down to a hundredth of it and takes the largest point that still clears the threshold. Every break-even figure below, and every interval bound, therefore carries the resolution of that grid — about three quarters of one percent of three times the campaign's highest daily spend, a dollar or two a day on these campaigns — and not the cent they are printed to. A bound sitting at the top of the grid is “above anything observed”, not an estimate. When a campaign's spend barely moved, no curve can be fitted, and the engine says so instead of guessing.

Claims and their windows

Four exports were reconciled against what Amazon had already paid: inventory adjustments, reimbursements, customer returns and settlements. The engine found 15 possible claims. 11 can be filed now: $340.27 at face value, $252.29 after the engine's approval assumptions. 1 is not yet eligible, and 3 had already closed, worth $88.46. 3 of the fileable claims close within 13 days of the run.

How to read this

Windows and approval odds are the engine's reading of Amazon's policy, not verified against Amazon. Warehouse losses open 30 days after the event and close at 60; refunds without a return open at 60 and close at 120; damaged returns close at 60. A claim counts as proven on the Profit Record only when Amazon pays it. A claim actually filed counts as found at its expected value from the day it is filed (value.py, IDENTIFIED_CLAIM_STATES = (“filed”,)), and found dollars count toward the bar an invoice is measured against. Found dollars do not expire in code today, so clearing stale ones before an invoice is the founder's own check. Inbound shipment shortages need an export this run did not have.

Elasticity

Coverage. All 23 of 23 intervals contain the planted value. That is less than it sounds. The intervals are wide (median width 4.57), and 21 of the 23 include −1, so they rarely say which side of break-even a SKU is on. Before pooling, the raw per-SKU intervals contained the truth 19 of 23 times. The four raw misses:

  • TH-SPICE-JAR-24: planted −1.80, raw fit 0.25 (−1.73 to 2.23).
  • TH-SPICE-JAR-12: planted −2.50, raw fit −5.57 (−7.97 to −3.17).
  • TH-CUT-BOARD-XL: planted −1.05, raw fit −5.58 (−9.54 to −1.63).
  • TH-FOOD-CONT-10: planted −2.00, raw fit −5.06 (−8.03 to −2.08).

The bias. The value the moves used reads demand as more price-sensitive than planted, by a median −0.71; only 4 of 23 read less sensitive. The cause was traced on the generator's own demand with the random shocks removed. The engine's regression has no season term, and in these months the Black Friday and Prime Day deals and the price tests fell in the seasonal peaks, so low prices kept company with high-season demand. The same regression without a season term is off by a median −0.258; with one, 0.000. Pooling toward the catalogue's −2.12 pulled the price-insensitive SKUs further the same way.

The engine's documented bias from reactive pricing runs the other way, toward reading demand as less sensitive (MATH_METHODS.md §10, item 1). Here the season outweighed it. Its list of limits does not name seasonality. This run says it should.

How to read this

An interval that contains the truth is only useful if it is narrow enough to act on. Read the width before the coverage.

The other did — TH-FOOD-CONT-10's planted −⁠$269 sits inside its −⁠$335 to +⁠$1,875 — but it contained the truth while pointing the wrong way.

The steps without a figure are worth +⁠$2,181.48 a period together under the planted curves. Three lose money: TH-CUT-BOARD-XL −⁠$293, TH-UNDER-SINK −⁠$60, TH-DRAWER-ORG-6 −⁠$29.

The refusals. Standing still is not free either. Under the planted curves a 5% step in the right direction was worth +⁠$261 a period on TH-BAG-CLIP-20 and +⁠$72 on TH-FOOD-CONT-5.

A documented claim that did not hold. MATH_METHODS.md §2 says the SKUs the engine declines to price carry more error than the ones it quotes. Here the 2 quoted SKUs averaged 1.61 absolute error and the other 21 averaged 0.94. Two SKUs cannot test the claim either way.

Engine defects this run exposed

Synthetic catalogue, real engine, not a client result

Six behaviours in the engine's code that this run exposed, in plain terms. Six is what one synthetic catalogue surfaced, not a count of what is there. They are reported as of git b3b2bd6 and were not fixed before this page was built. The wording error in the claims move is listed under Claims.

How to read this

These are defects in code, not limits of the method. Each has evidence from this run and, where noted, a reproduction on a plain series.

E1. Daily ad spend alerts can contradict themselves.
When a campaign's budget changed early in the file, the change detector takes the direction from the first run to cross its threshold, but measures the shift from that run to the end of the file. On this run 4 rows say spend “fell” while their own numbers rise. Because a dollar figure is computed only for rises, a real spend increase never becomes a move. Reproduced on a plain 92-day series; a control with one late change comes out right.
E2. A Buy Box drop can never become a move from the weekly sweep.
The flag carries no dollar figure, and flags become moves only at $100 or more.
E3. Two break-even intervals misbehave: one excludes its own estimate, one is bounded by it.
On SP - Spice Jars - Exact the fitted curve sits against the lower bound of its shape parameter (h = 0.51, bound 0.5), so the Monte Carlo draws that build the interval are cut off on one side: ad_efficiency discards any draw with a shape outside 0.5 to 3.0. The estimate is $68.44 a day; the interval runs $263.53 to $353.90. A fit like this is meant to be reported as unavailable; this one was not caught. No trim was drafted, which the planted curve happens to agree with. The same interval machinery misbehaves a second way on this run, and on the row carrying the largest ad figure on the page: on SP - Storage - Broad the top of the 90% interval is the point estimate itself, $163.21, so the interval is entirely one-sided and the trim is not in fact sized short of the estimate, as the Ads section says trims are. The $1,004.67 rests on that. Both rows are also grid quantities rather than solved ones — see the break-even note under Ads.
E4. The engine's report template disagrees with its own moves.
Rendered on this run, the template's list of moves leaves out the claims, fee-line, fee-change and branded moves, and quotes different purchase orders for the same SKUs. TH-FOOD-CONT-10 reads $27,796 for 2,206 units in the template and $20,677 for 1,641 units in the drafted move. The template sizes reorders by a flat rule; the move uses the service level the SKU's margin justifies. This page shows the drafted moves. The template also tells the client those quantities are “sized for a 95% service level”. They are not: inventory_sim sizes them as mean demand across the lead time plus a fixed 30 days of cover, the same flat rule for every SKU whatever its margin. That sentence is wrong in the client's own copy, and the template prints only the first fifteen SKUs.
E5. A reversal was counted as a claim.
A reimbursement Amazon reversed because the units were found is listed as live at a 50% approval assumption, and its $8.90 is counted in the filing move before the manual check the code's own documentation promises.
E6. The bleed-term move counts rows, not terms.
The move drafting sums each ad row's list of zero-sale terms, so a term that appears in two export periods is counted twice, and the sentence a client reads announces that count as terms. On this run the client-facing move said “21 search terms” when 13 exist across 21 term-periods. The dollar total, $3,477.88, is unaffected: it sums spend, not names. Like the claims-move wording error under Claims, this one reaches a client's inbox, not just an internal payload.

The one change after the first run

Synthetic catalogue, real engine, not a client result

After the first run, one label in the answer key was corrected, and the correction favours the engine. Refund R5 on TH-DRAWER-ORG-6 was refunded 2026-08-08, 36 days before the run date. A note written before any engine run said it was too early to know whether the item would come back, but the generator's code labelled it “not yet eligible”, a known debt not yet fileable. The label was changed to “too recent to judge”, which matches the engine's rule of not judging refunds under 45 days old.

No data row changed: the exports are byte-identical and the engine's outputs did not change. The grade did. Run one marked R5 as a miss; it now reads “not surfaced, by design”. The first run's outputs are kept for anyone who wants the before state.

The run script also gained checks after run one, written to catch problems: whether each planted shift was dated right and became a move, whether each break-even estimate lies inside its own interval, whether the report template matches the drafted moves, and how much of the elasticity error the season explains. None of them alters an engine output or a planted value.

How to read this

The rule for this test: no change after seeing engine output may make the engine look better, except a fix to a data or label bug, logged with its effect. This is the only one.

What was synthetic: all of it

  • The brand, its 24 SKUs and every figure: prices, demand, traffic, Buy Box share, fees, landed costs, lead times, stock, the ad account, returns, refunds and reimbursements. The cash inputs the cash model used ($240,000 on hand, $34,000 a month fixed) were invented too, and are not shown above.
  • Every Amazon identifier in the files — ASINs, FNSKUs, order IDs — starts with SYN, so none can match a real Amazon record. The SKU codes shown above (TH-…) were invented for this catalogue too. Each file opens with a line saying it is synthetic.
  • The files copy the layout of Amazon's reports so the engine's own parsers read them as they would read a client's upload.
  • Storage rates of $0.78 and $2.40 per cubic foot and a 15% referral rate are synthetic choices matching the engine's fallback schedule, not a statement of Amazon's current fees.

Where the planted world is harder than the engine assumes

  • Prices react to demand: every SKU had its list price changed on recent demand in 2026-01 and 2026-05, up or down by 4%.
  • Deals fall in the seasonal peaks: Black Friday in 2025-11 (season index 1.22) and Prime Day in 2026-07 (1.08); December runs at 1.30.
  • Lead-time spread is 0.25 against the engine's assumed 0.2, and Q4 seasonality is in the forward demand.
  • An inbound placement fee is exported under “Other Fees”, which the engine treats as a share of price.

Where it is kinder than reality

  • Constant elasticity really is the true demand curve, and ad response really is a Hill curve.
  • No competitors, no cross-price effects, no traffic lift from deal badges, and no lost sales hidden by stockouts in the history.
  • Fees are exact. The payments export carries refunds, storage and subscription lines only, and the inventory events export carries receipts and adjustments only; no model output shown here reads the lines left out.
  • The fees in the exports came from the engine's own fallback schedule, including its flat 15% referral rate, so anything the engine has wrong about real referral rates could not show up here: it applies one flat rate everywhere, and we have not checked that schedule against Seller Central or against a dated Amazon page. A Shopify weight-rounding defect we know about could not show up either, because this is an Amazon-only catalogue.

What departed from production

  • An in-memory database built on the engine's own test double, in place of the live database.
  • File storage read from local disk, in place of cloud storage.
  • Today's date pinned to the run date inside the engine.

No engine source was edited and no network service was used. At run time the engine's working tree had uncommitted changes by someone else in 7 files; none is a model, a parser, the move drafting or the report. A clean checkout of b3b2bd6 has not been tested.

The name was checked by web search on 2026-09-13: no brand, product, seller or trademark called Tarnhollow turned up. A web search is not a trademark search. If the name is yours, write to us and it will be changed.
