What day 30 will probably look like, plainly

A move is graded once an export covering the time after it has arrived (section 4.1 has the detail). If your SKU Economics export is monthly, a price step made in week two is usually graded on the following month's export, after day 30. So at day 30, much of the bar is likely to be found (moves actually made, counted at their expected dollars) rather than proven.

Found is not a free pass, with one gap still open. When a found move is graded, what it actually earned replaces what was expected, even if that number is lower or negative. The bar for every later invoice counts everything since day 1, so a move that disappoints in month two shows up in the bar for month two. But a move that cannot be graded stays found at its expected dollars, and the code sets no time limit on that today. Two examples: a price step on a SKU that sold nothing afterwards, and any move after which no new export arrives. Shown on a call

2. What happens to a file when it lands

A file passes five steps before any model sees it. At every step, "no" is one of the possible answers, and a no always comes with its reason. The code for all five is in our private repository; we will open any of it on the call.

Step 1. In your browser, before anything uploads

Your browser reads the first lines of each file and checks them against the same required columns the parser will demand. A file that is clearly a different report is stopped, with a message naming the card it belongs on, because a file on the wrong card uploads cleanly, parses into nothing, and costs a day to discover. A file that is only missing a column gets a warning and uploads if you say so: the check can be wrong, and you know your file. Each file can be up to 50 MB, and a Shopify orders export bigger than that can be sent a month at a time. Shown on a call

Step 2. Reading the file

The file is decoded whichever way it was saved: UTF-8 with or without a byte-order mark, or the older Windows encodings some accounts still export. The delimiter is detected (comma, tab or semicolon), and prose lines above the real header are skipped. If no header appears in the first 20 lines, or there is a header with no rows under it, the file is refused with that reason. Shown on a call

Step 3. Parsing

There is one parser per report type: 18 of them across Amazon, Shopify, Meta and Google Ads, the cost sheet included. Each finds the columns it needs by name. If a required column is missing, the file is refused, and the refusal lists the headers the file actually had. A date format the parser does not recognize is refused, not guessed. Trailer rows that are totals ("Total: Account") are dropped, and only those: a campaign named "Total Store" stays. A file that parses to zero usable rows is marked failed with the reason. Before your Teardown, Hagen sees it in the hourly digest; after it, in the Monday digest. Shown on a call

A Shopify orders export carries your customers' names, emails and addresses, because that is how Shopify stores an order. The parser reads ten columns (order name, financial status, created and cancelled dates, refunded amount, and each line item's SKU, name, quantity, price and discount) and writes a per-SKU monthly total to our database. No customer name, email, address, phone number or payment detail is written to that database. The file itself, whether you upload it or Hagen downloads it through the seat, is kept as sent in an encrypted, access-controlled storage bucket, and deleted with the rest of your data when you ask (see the privacy policy). You may delete the customer columns before uploading; the models never read them. Shown on a call

Step 4. Blanks stay blank

A blank cost is recorded as blank, never as zero. A SKU with no cost on file gets no profit figure, no best price and no dollar promise, and the move says what is missing: "Upload unit costs". Shown on a call

Step 5. Enough data, or a named refusal

Every model checks it has enough to go on before it produces a number. When it does not, the output is a status that says why. "Not enough data" is a result we deliver, not a gap we paper over.

3. How a move is made

The tests named in this section are in our private repository. We will run any of them with you on the call.

3.1 What a move carries, before it goes live

Before a move goes live, it is written down with:

  • the dollars it is expected to earn per period;
  • a 90% range around that figure;
  • the chance it goes the other way. This is never printed as 0%. The floor is "under 1%", because zero bad outcomes among 6,000 simulated ones is not proof there are none;
  • every input that made the promise: the fitted demand curve and its uncertainty, the baseline units, revenue, fees and cost, and the simulation's fixed seed. That is what lets the move be graded later against the same numbers that made the promise, not against a later and different fit.

The expected dollars are the median of 6,000 simulated outcomes drawn across every uncertain input. They are not the single best-guess figure, though that figure is kept beside it. Shown on a call

Some moves carry no dollar figure, on purpose: a reorder (a stockout that did not happen cannot be measured), a price step where the data cannot locate the best price, and a price test on a SKU with no cost on file. We do not print a figure now that we could not grade later. Shown on a call

3.2 Which moves reach you, and how many

At most five moves per email, highest expected dollars first. The rest are not queued. They are checked again at the next Monday pass and reach you then if the data still supports them, so you are never handed a wall of twelve decisions. Shown on a call

3.3 Your standing yes, and what needs your written yes

On the kickoff call you set your standing yes: the moves we make without asking again. Until it is recorded, it is the default the terms print. That covers price steps of up to 5% per SKU per two-week cycle, and ad corrections: negative-matching terms that spend without converting, bid and budget changes, and pausing what loses money. Inventory orders, listing changes, bigger price moves, new campaigns and filing a claim in your account all wait for your written yes. Check it yourself in the terms, section 6.

One default ad move sits at the edge of that wording: pausing exact-match branded search targeting as a tracked test, because part of what those ads sell would likely have sold anyway. The code treats it as inside the default standing yes, although it is not shown to lose money before it is paused. If you want it to need your written yes, say so at kickoff. The code can only move all ad moves to a written yes together, so for this one alone Hagen holds it back by hand. Shown on a call for the default; Our word for holding it back.

If you take a whole kind of move out of your standing yes (all price steps, say), every move of that kind is sent to you for a written yes instead of being approved by silence. Shown on a call If you keep a kind but narrow its size (price steps of 3% instead of 5%), the narrower limit is recorded, but the code does not yet check steps against it. Hagen checks each step against it by hand and sends anything larger for your written yes. Our word

One more check sits on top. If a move's simulated bad case (its 5th-percentile outcome) would lose more than 15% of that SKU's own monthly profit, it goes to you for a written yes whatever its size. In practice the step sizing below already refuses such moves, so this check is a backstop. Shown on a call

3.4 The email at least three days ahead, and a reply window that cannot open on an unsent email

The Monday pass drafts the moves and sends one email listing them. The order inside the code is the point. The email goes first. Only if our email provider accepts the send does the 72-hour reply window open, and only for the moves listed in that email. If the send fails, the moves stay visible in Hubricon, your sign-in, but the window never opens, so no amount of silence can become a yes. The code cannot see a bounce or a spam folder once the provider has accepted the email. Shown on a call

To stop a move, press Decline beside it in Hubricon. That is recorded the moment you press it. Shown on a call You can also reply no to the email, as the terms allow. No code reads replies, so an emailed no stops a move once Hagen records it, which he does by hand before the next Monday pass. Our word

A test sends a batch with email switched off. It checks that no window opens and that nothing is ever approved, however long the moves sit. Shown on a call

At the first Monday pass after the window closes, moves inside your standing yes that nobody stopped are approved. That is about a week after the email, not the moment the 72 hours end. A move outside it is never approved by silence. After 21 days without an answer it lapses, and its note says silence is not consent. Shown on a call

3.5 How far a price step goes: solved for, not capped

A price step is the size the data supports, not a fixed 5%. For each SKU the engine tries candidate prices at 0.25% intervals and picks the one with the best expected gain after a penalty for how uncertain that gain is. Three limits apply:

  • the average of the worst 5% of simulated outcomes may not lose more than 15% of that SKU's own monthly profit;
  • the step never exceeds the 5% your terms allow;
  • the step never walks past the best price.

Standing still is always one of the candidates, and it scores zero. A SKU where no step beats standing still gets no step. Shown on a call

The limit of that claim, plainly: across twelve simulated runs and 703 moves, 71.8% of steps still land on the 5% limit. The statistics set the size of about 28% of steps. The limit does most of the work. Simulated Shown on a call

One thing is deliberately not modeled. Nothing in any export prices the risk that a bigger step loses the Buy Box, because no competitor price series reaches the engine. A curve for that risk would make the math look finished and would be invented. The 5% limit, and a person watching the live step, carry that risk instead. Shown on a call

3.6 When we will not name a price

Every demand curve has a point where a price change exactly pays for itself: an elasticity of −1. Near that point, the formula for the best price runs off toward infinity. The multiplier ε/(1+ε) is 21 at −1.05 and 51 at −1.02, so two estimates three hundredths apart name prices more than twice apart. Check it yourself

So when a SKU's 95% range touches −1, or its estimate sits within two standard errors of it, the engine quotes no best price and no dollar figure. You get a small step, and the move says so in these words: "We cannot separate this SKU's demand from the break-even point where a price move pays for itself … so there is no destination to quote and no dollar figure we would stand behind." Shown on a call

How often: on one simulated catalog of 120 SKUs with seven months of history and 22% demand noise, 101 of 117 price steps came back this way. Simulated Shown on a call

3.7 Watched while it is live

Your terms say a price step is watched while it is live, through Buy Box share on Amazon or conversion rate on Shopify, and reversed if it costs you the sale. Check it yourself in the terms, section 6.

How that works, plainly: the watching and the stepping back are done by Hagen, by hand. No code steps a price back on its own, and there is no automatic alarm on a fall in Shopify conversion. Our word

What the code adds comes later, at grading. If your Business Report shows Buy Box share 10 points or more lower after the step than before it, that is written into the move's row, and the step is recorded at its measured value, loss included, when the movement is $25 or more. A smaller movement closes under the $25 floor with nothing banked, and the Buy Box note stays in the row. Shown on a call

3.8 Every price step can be undone next cycle

Every price step the engine drafts is at most 5% before rounding to the cent, so reversing it is inside the same authority and needs no new signature. The price before the step is on the record, so the undo is exact. A test checks both. Shown on a call The code does not yet stop a second step on the same SKU inside two weeks once the first has been graded; Hagen holds that line by hand. Our word

3.9 An example promise Hypothetical

Hypothetical SKU · illustrative arithmetic · not a client and not a result

Last month: 2,000 units at $24.00. Landed cost $7.00 a unit. Referral fee 15% of price. FBA fee $4.50 a unit. Profit before ads: 2,000 × ($24.00 × 0.85 − $11.50) = 2,000 × $8.90 = $17,800. Check it yourself

Fitted elasticity −2.0, standard error 0.25, 95% range −2.56 to −1.44 (with 10 degrees of freedom, as on twelve months of history, the multiplier is 2.228). The range is clear of −1, so a best price can be named:

P* = [(c + F) ÷ (1 − f)] × ε ÷ (1 + ε) = [($7.00 + $4.50) ÷ 0.85] × (−2.0 ÷ −1.0) = $27.06. Check it yourself

That is 12.7% above today's price, so the step stops at the 5% limit: $24.00 → $25.20. At an elasticity of −2.0, units would be about 2,000 × 1.05−2 = 1,814, and profit 1,814 × ($25.20 × 0.85 − $11.50) = $17,995. That is $195 more than last month. Check it yourself

The move the engine wrote on these hypothetical inputs:

"Move HYPOTHETICAL-SKU $24.00 → $25.20 (+$1.20; optimum $27.06). Expected +$195/period (90% range −$209 to +$608; a 19% chance it goes the other way). Run as a tracked test — Buy Box watched while the step is live."

The engine marked it inside the standing yes. The step is within 5%, and its bad case ($209 at the 5th percentile) is well inside 15% of this SKU's trailing monthly profit. The engine restates last month (31 days) to 30: $17,800 × 30 ÷ 31 = $17,226, and 15% of that is $2,584. Check it yourself The $195 in the move is the median of the simulation ($195.36), which sits beside the single best-guess figure ($195.46). The range and the 19% chance come from the simulation. The last sentence of the move is the engine's standard wording; 3.7 says who does the watching. Shown on a call

4. How a dollar is graded

Afterwards, every move is graded from your own later exports. The grade says how we know, and a set of rules decides what may be counted. Most rules in this section can only lower a number or leave it alone. Two can also take a loss off the Record: the $25 floor closes a miss smaller than $25 with nothing banked, and one dollar, one move sets aside a second reading of a movement already credited to another move, whether that reading was a gain or a loss. Each says so below. The grading code and its tests are in our private repository; we will open them on the call.

4.1 Before grading: was it done, and is there an export after it

Grading starts only when two things are true.

The move was actually made. If the price in your next export is still the old one (within 2%), the step is marked "not made" and nothing is measured. If it is still not made 90 days on, it closes as never carried out.

An export covers the time after the move. Negative-matched search terms, ad spend corrections and branded-search pauses are not graded on less than 14 days of exports after the move. Price steps and margin moves are graded on the export periods that start after the move, whatever their length. Fee corrections are graded on the latest reading after the move. If no export has arrived yet, the row says "not yet", because a zero would record a miss we have no evidence for. Two kinds of move can record a zero early: a campaign trim is read against the latest ad report, and if spend has not fallen yet it is recorded at $0 and not graded again; a fee correction whose fee is still at the flagged level is recorded the same way. Shown on a call

4.3 The rules that decide what counts

1. Capped at the promise. If an isolated or attributable result comes in above what we expected, it is banked at what we expected. The excess is written in the row and never added to the total. A miss of $25 or more is banked in full (rule 2 covers smaller ones). Claims are the one exception, because a claim pays what Amazon pays. The cap is applied in the single step every grade passes through, so a new kind of move cannot skip it.

2. A $25 floor. A movement under $25 either way closes with nothing banked, because at that size it is noise. That includes a loss under $25: it is written in the row, and not counted against the Record. The floor is applied to every grade at the end of each pass.

3. Graded once, on exports after the move. Negative-matched terms, spend corrections and branded pauses need at least 14 days of exports after the move. Price steps and margin moves are graded over the export periods that start after the move, whatever their length. A fee correction is graded on the latest reading in your export, so a fee that crept back up before grading is graded as nothing recovered. Each move is graded once, and nothing checks that a gain lasted beyond the window it was graded on.

4. One dollar, one move. When two moves touch the same SKU in the same period, the movement is credited once, to the older move. The other move's reading is set aside as unmeasurable, whether it was a gain or a loss, and its row says where the dollars went. Shown on a call for rules 1 to 4

Price steps get three more.

5. The cautious reading. The result is not graded at our single best guess. It is graded across the whole fitted range, with 8,000 draws and a fixed seed so that re-grading gives the same number, and the Record banks the 25th percentile. That is a deliberately cautious point for grading. The step itself was not sized at this percentile; section 3.5 describes how it was sized.

6. Never more than your profit actually rose. The credit cannot exceed the change in that SKU's own profit against its baseline. That before-and-after change includes the season and everything else, which is why it is never what gets banked. As a ceiling, though, it can only lower a claim.

7. A lost Buy Box stays on the record. If Buy Box share fell 10 points or more across the step, that is written into the row, and the step is recorded at its measured value, loss included, when the movement is $25 or more. Under $25 the floor in rule 2 still closes it with nothing banked, and the note stays in the row. Shown on a call for rules 5 to 7

The result of all of this: a correct forecast still banks less than it promised. On simulated sellers whose demand behaved exactly as fitted, the Record banked about 0.6 of the promise. Simulated Shown on a call

4.4 Found, and proven

A dollar on the Profit Record is in one of two states.

Proven. Graded on your own later exports, or paid by Amazon.

Found, not yet banked. A move actually made in your account, not yet graded, counted at its expected dollars, with no time limit today (section 1). Or a claim actually filed with Amazon and not yet paid. That is all. A move that was only proposed does not count. A claim that was only spotted in a report does not count. A claim whose window expired, or that Amazon denied, is neither found nor proven.

An invoice can stand only if proven plus found, counted since the day you said yes, is more than everything billed since then, that invoice included. More than, not equal to: a tie goes to you. Check it yourself for the rule, in the terms, section 3. Shown on a call for the code.

When results are one day published, the dollar figures on them will be proven dollars only, never found. Shown on a call One kind of result, marking that the Record cleared the day-30 bar, records an event that found dollars helped decide, and it will say so where it appears. Our word

5. How the engine checks itself

Simulated Every measured figure in this section comes from simulated sellers: synthetic data we generated, not any client account. It tells you how the method behaves. It is not a result. The tests and simulation runs are in our private repository. We will run the tests on a call; the larger simulation studies take longer than a call, so for those we send the saved output.

5.1 The tests

At the commit this page describes (12 September 2026), the engine carried 979 automated tests, and all 979 passed when we ran them on 13 September 2026. 209 of them were added in the math work described below: 199 in fifteen new test files, and 10 more in three existing ones. Shown on a call

5.2 Do the published ranges contain the truth? Simulated

  • The range on how demand responds to price. Across 1,000 simulated SKUs per setting, the published 95% range contained the true value 94.4% to 96.6% of the time, at five and twelve months of history, with noise both even and uneven. The classical standard error, with the same multiplier, applied to the same simulated data under uneven noise, falls to 90.9%. Simulated Shown on a call
  • Why the range is wider than a spreadsheet's. With five months of history, the correct multiplier for a 95% range is 3.182, not 1.96. Using 1.96 would print a range 38% too narrow on exactly the thinnest SKUs: 1.96 ÷ 3.182 = 0.62. Check it yourself
  • The range on a move's expected dollars. On 1,000 simulated SKUs, the published 90% range contained the realized result 91.6% to 94.0% of the time at seven months of history, and 96.0% to 96.7% at five. At five months the range errs wide, which makes steps smaller than they need to be. That costs upside rather than adding risk. Simulated Shown on a call
  • Borrowing from your catalog. A SKU with little history borrows strength from the rest of your catalog, in proportion to how noisy its own estimate is. Across 30 simulated sellers of 20 SKUs each, that beat each SKU's own estimate overall, and for at least 20 of the 30 sellers individually. You see both numbers and the weight. Simulated Shown on a call
  • The best-price formula. A symbolic algebra check confirms the formula's single root, and a 200,001-point search confirms it at four elasticities and four fee structures. Shown on a call; the formula itself, in 3.9: Check it yourself

5.3 Thousands of checks, and noise Simulated

Each week the engine runs change detection over every fee line, traffic metric, campaign and settlement series. On a real catalog that is thousands of checks. Run a threshold thousands of times and noise will cross it.

  • On 500 simulated SKUs of pure noise, with nothing real planted, the detectors on their own flagged 548 of 4,000 checks. After controlling for how many checks were run, none survived.
  • A real $1.20-a-unit fee change planted among those 500 noise SKUs still survived, with its $120-a-period figure intact. The control removes noise, not findings.
  • On a simulated 120-SKU catalog: 960 checks, 24 raw flags, 0 findings reaching the seller. Nothing real was planted there, so 0 is the right answer.

The engine records how many checks stood behind each weekly pass, and your report says its fee-change alerts are controlled for running thousands of checks. The alert itself does not print that count yet. Simulated for the figures above; Shown on a call for the rest.

5.4 Scoring our own promises against what happened

A separate check re-grades a client's whole history of moves against their exports and asks three questions. Are we banking a sensible share of what we promised? The target is 0.30 to 1.30, and anything below 0.30 is labelled over-promising. Did the results land inside the ranges we published at least 80% of the time? And can every promise be rebuilt from what was written down? It also reports drift: a banked grade that no longer reproduces. Until at least 8 moves have been graded it says "pending", not a ratio. Shown on a call

The first time it ran, on simulated histories, it caught two errors in our own grading (section 6, item 10). It has never run on a client's history, because there is none yet. Until it does, every calibration number on this page describes a simulation. Shown on a call

5.5 Does the careful step earn its keep? A split result Simulated

We raced five pricing rules on 40 simulated sellers of 25 SKUs each, in three settings, including one where each SKU's true demand drifts over time. Scoring was per move, over a thousand moves.

  • Every rule that uses the fitted demand curve beat a flat 3% step by more than 50% on total profit.
  • The careful step we use did not beat the simpler "go straight for the best guess" rule on total profit. It was 1–2% behind in every setting, because it declines moves the simpler rule takes. Per move, the two were a dead heat.
  • It did win on bad outcomes, in every setting: the worst tenth of moves were 10–18% less bad, and dollars lost on losing moves were 13–18% lower.

Because this is a split result and a simulation cannot settle it, our own scorecard gives it 9 out of 10, not 10. Only real client history can settle it. Simulated Shown on a call

5.6 Same inputs, same answer

The whole weekly cycle is run twice on the same catalog, and every output is checked for an exact match. Re-grading a move reproduces what it banked, to the cent. The randomness in every simulation is seeded, so no re-run can move a figure you were already given. Shown on a call

6. What we got wrong, and withdrew

A method that only publishes its successes is asking you to trust it. This section lists what our own checks caught, in plain terms: what we said, what was actually true, and what changed. Each correction is dated, and was written into the documents where the original claim was made. Nothing here reached a client, because there are none yet, which is also why we fixed these now. The only account on file is our own internal test account, and none of its moves was emailed to anyone or carried out. The commits named are in our private repository; we will open any of them on the call.

1. A guard that could be switched off by the answer it was guarding against

Fixed 12 September 2026

The check that refuses to quote a best price near −1 (section 3.6) treated a missing uncertainty figure as a claim of certainty. So a fit whose uncertainty came back infinite sailed through. Measured: a SKU with an estimate of −1.05 got a best price of $123.53 on a $20 item, a full 5% step, a dollar promise, and "under a 1% chance it goes the other way". That is maximum confidence at the exact point where there is no information.

Now a fitted estimate with no usable uncertainty is refused. The test that had approved the old behavior now asserts the opposite, with the measurement written into it. Four test fixtures that had left out the uncertainty were made realistic; the rule was not weakened to make them pass. Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

2. One screen showed a dollar figure the move itself refused

Fixed 12 September 2026

On price moves whose own text said there was "no dollar figure we would stand behind", the charts inside Hubricon still printed an expected dollar figure. The engine must not refuse a number in one place and publish it in another. The charts now follow the move. Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

3. Unequal period lengths bent the demand estimate

Fixed 12 September 2026

Units were compared across periods without adjusting for how many days each period had. For a 5% step held 15 days against a 16-day baseline, that shifts the estimate by −1.32. At 14 days against 16, by −2.74. That is 1.3 to 2.7 points of elasticity, pointing toward "cut the price". A calendar fortnight inside a 31-day month forces 15 against 16, so asking clients for fortnightly exports, the obvious way to sharpen the estimate, would have shipped a bias. Units are now converted to a daily rate first. On equal periods nothing changes, which is exactly why this looked like a nicety.

Check it yourself ln(15/16) ÷ ln(1.05) = −1.32, and ln(14/16) ÷ ln(1.05) = −2.74. The fix: Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

4. A reassuring number that was an accident of eight runs

Withdrawn 12 September 2026

We had reported that part of the pricing bias (section 7, item 1) was a harmless +0.15 small-sample effect, separate from the dangerous part. There was only one thing. Twelve independent 8-run batches of that simulated setting ranged from −0.117 to +0.145, and pooled over 96 runs the figure is +0.022. The test that pinned +0.15 passed 5 of those 12 batches. So the whole +0.51 bias in the simulated reactive-pricing setting is the dangerous kind: the problem is bigger than we had said, not smaller. A new test asserts that 8 runs cannot measure this setting, so the mistake cannot recur. Simulated Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

5. A safety claim withdrawn the same morning it was committed

Withdrawn 12 September 2026

We added a line to the client report saying that a quoted price is always at or above the true best price, so it is safe to treat as a ceiling. The math behind it holds in very long histories. But on the estimate the engine actually uses, for the SKUs it actually quotes, it held for 79.7% of SKUs in one simulated setting and 37.3% in another, both inside the claim's own stated conditions. A bound that is wrong for a fifth to nearly two-thirds of the SKUs it is printed on is not a bound. The report now makes a catalog-level statement that names its own limits, and a test asserts the per-SKU claim stays gone. Simulated Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

6. "Our own price steps fix the bias." They do not.

Withdrawn 12 September 2026

The client report said every step we make is a clean price experiment, so the data would get better on its own. That was false for two reasons, both measured. First, we choose each step from the same fitted curve, which is shaped by the same demand swings that bias it, so the step is not independent of demand. Second, a 14-day price test inside a monthly export averages away to almost nothing: it leaves price variation of 1.1%, under the 2% the engine needs, so it teaches the estimate nothing at all. A step held for a whole month does show (2.4%), and a later pass narrowed the withdrawal to say so. The first reason still stands. Simulated Shown on a call commit ec101f7

7. An arithmetic error that halved the danger

Corrected 12 September 2026

Our methods document said an estimate of −1.6, when the truth is −2.0, puts the best price 17% too high. It is 33.3%. The test meant to pin the figure only checked "more than 15%", which is how the error survived. It now pins 4/3 exactly. Check it yourself (1.6 ÷ 0.6) ÷ (2.0 ÷ 1.0) = 4/3. commit ec101f7

8. Step-size figures from a single run

Corrected 12 September 2026

We reported that the statistics, not the 5% limit, set 43% of step sizes. Over twelve simulated runs and 703 moves the figure is about 28%, with 71.8% of steps on the limit. A quarter of steps are 4.5% or smaller, not 3.0% or smaller as we had said. Simulated Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

9. A correction we designed, tested, and did not ship

Not shipped 12 September 2026

We designed a way to subtract the pricing bias using only the data already on file, then set a second pass the job of overturning it. It was overturned. The correction only worked because of an error in one of its inputs, so improving that input broke it. On a mixed simulated catalog its headline number looked like total success, with the typical bias going from +0.431 to −0.011. Meanwhile overall error rose 197%, 42% of SKUs were made worse, and the new errors pointed toward "cut the price". It stays unbuilt. One small piece survived: a measure of how strongly a seller reprices in response to last month's demand. That piece is under measurement and not yet claimed. Simulated Shown on a call commit b3b2bd6

10. Two grading errors, caught by the check in 5.4

Fixed 11 September 2026

We had changed how the demand estimate's range is computed, making it honestly wider. The grading rule still took the worst end of that range, and the replay check caught the effect: on 24 simulated price cuts that all made money, the grading booked 18 as losses. Taking the extreme end of a wide range is not caution; it is a wrong answer. The rule became the 25th percentile. The same check caught a second error: a price cut followed by no change in volume was still booked as a gain. The claim is now capped at what the SKU's profit actually did. Simulated Shown on a call commit 45c216c

7. What the engine cannot tell you

If you read one section, read this one.

  1. Whether a price change caused what followed. Demand is estimated from prices you chose, often in response to how sales were running. That makes demand look less price-sensitive than it is. In our simulations of sellers who reprice off last month's numbers, the error was about +0.4 to +0.5 of an elasticity, in the direction that argues for raising prices. It is not corrected, and with the data in an Amazon or Shopify export it cannot be. Our own price steps do not fix it either (section 6, item 6). Simulated Shown on a call
  2. A best price for most SKUs on a first upload. With seven months of history, 10% price variation and 22% demand noise, about five SKUs in six get no best price. They still get a direction and a step. Simulated Shown on a call
  3. Cannibalization between your own SKUs. A cut on one SKU that takes sales from another shows up as a win on the first and an unexplained loss on the second. Our word
  4. What your competitors are doing. No competitor price, assortment or stock data reaches the engine. Your Buy Box share is the only shadow of it. Our word
  5. The value of a stockout you did not have. Sales you would have lost cannot be observed, so a reorder carries no dollar promise, and the Record proves only that the order landed. Shown on a call
  6. Whether an ad drove the SKU it is charged to. Ad spend is split across SKUs by their share of revenue. That is accounting, not attribution. Ad results also inherit the attribution window of the Amazon ad report they come from. Our word
  7. Why a detected shift happened. "Fees rose in May" is a measurement. "Because the listing was re-measured" is a guess, and a move states it as one. Our word
  8. Anything about your cash you did not tell us. Cash on hand and fixed costs are inputs you give us. The runway is a simulated balance crossing zero: a bridge-capital line, never a bankruptcy prediction. Our word
  9. Whether the shapes of the curves are right. Every range on this page assumes a particular curve shape: constant elasticity for demand, a saturating curve for ads, lognormal demand, one shared demand factor. No amount of resampling tests the shape itself. Our word

The assumption that would cost you most if it is wrong is the first one. The size is not the danger; the direction is. An engine that nudges prices up, on evidence that understates how much volume a rise will cost, could take months of exports to catch, and would look like ordinary seasonality while it happened. Four things stand between that and your account:

  • no best price is quoted where the estimate cannot be separated from −1, and the most-biased SKUs land there disproportionately;
  • the step is small when the fit is uncertain;
  • on Amazon, Hagen reads Buy Box share by hand while a step is live and takes back a step that costs it; on Shopify, where there is no Buy Box, he reads conversion rate instead. No code raises a same-day alarm on either on its own;
  • the Record banks the cautious 25th percentile, capped at what the SKU's profit actually did, so a move that did not work cannot be banked as one that did.

Shown on a call for the first, second and fourth; Our word for the third.

What would retire it, and why it is not built. Two things would have to change: a deliberately random part of each price step, independent of your data, and a price history fine enough to see a 14-day move. Neither exists today. Our word

Smaller limits, stated for completeness. Supplier lead-time spread is an assumption, because no export records actual lead times. Cost uncertainty is treated as zero, because your cost sheet is a number you state, not one we measure. Change detection assumes no slow trend or seasonality in a series, so a slow fee drift can read as a step. One shared demand factor describes a catalog with a seasonal line and an evergreen line only on average. Shown on a call
