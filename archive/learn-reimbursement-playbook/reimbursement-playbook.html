What Amazon owes you, and the reports it hides in

Amazon loses units, damages units, refunds customers who never send the item back, and grades some returns as its own fault. For each of those it owes you money. Since 1 November 2024 it pays most of that automatically, without a case. This playbook is about what the automatic pass misses or underpays, because that is where the money still sits.

On the demo catalogue this course runs on, Tarnhollow (demo data, 24 products, about $3.5M a year), the method finds 123 claims. 72 of them are open today, worth $4,593 at landed cost, or $3,573 once you apply honest approval odds. 37 of them close within 14 days. $825 has already been lost to windows that shut. Every one of those numbers came out of the same reports you can export tonight.

The reports

Everything lives in the seller's own exports. Nothing here needs a tool or an account you don't already have.

Report Where What it reveals
Inventory Ledger Reports → Fulfillment → Inventory Ledger units Amazon adjusted away, with a reason code and, sometimes, its own admission of the shortfall
FBA Customer Returns Reports → Fulfillment → Returns each return with the grade Amazon gave it
Reimbursements Reports → Fulfillment → Reimbursements what Amazon already paid, in cash or in replacement stock, and what it clawed back
Transactions Payments → Transaction View → download every refund, with the order it belongs to

The template

Download the spreadsheet under the video. It has a sheet for each report, helper columns that classify every row, a Claims sheet that dates and values each claim, and a filing log. Every window and approval assumption in it is the same constant Hubricon's own engine runs, so the template and the engine can never disagree. The rows already in it are demo data from the demo catalogue; delete them before you paste your own.

How a claim gets its number

Value is units multiplied by landed cost: unit cost plus inbound freight plus packaging. Amazon reimburses at manufacturing cost, so that is the honest figure. Without a cost sheet the template falls back to 50% of the selling price and says so. Expected value is that value multiplied by an approval probability that is stated, not hidden: 85% for warehouse losses, 70% for refunds without a return, 60% for returns Amazon damaged. Sort by expected value and days left, and you have the order to file in.

What this playbook does not cover

Inbound shipment shortages. They need the shipment reconciliation export, which is a different report with a different window. Lost units after a removal order. Fee overcharges. Each is real; none is in this course.

The next lessons take the claim families one at a time: where the row is, how to read it, how to value it, when it opens, when it shuts, and how to file.

Lost or damaged in the warehouse

This is the largest family on most accounts. On the demo catalogue it is 36 lost claims worth $3,232 and 20 damaged claims worth $1,324.

Where the row is

Open the Inventory Ledger. Filter Event Type to Adjustments. Every adjustment carries a Reason. The codes that mean Amazon lost your unit inside its own building are M and N. The codes that mean it damaged your unit are D and Q. A code of E is customer damage, which is not Amazon's liability, and the codes 5 and F mean a unit was found again. Amazon changes its code table between report generations, so the template also reads the reason text: anything that says misplaced, lost or unrecoverable is a loss; anything that says damaged is damage.

Read Amazon's own admission first

Some ledger rows carry an Unreconciled Quantity. That is Amazon stating, in its own report, that the units are missing and unexplained. When it is present, use it as the claim quantity. It is the strongest evidence you will ever have, because the counterparty wrote it.

Without it, the claim quantity is the adjustment quantity, made positive.

Net out what was already fixed

Net out what was already fixed before you file. Filing without netting is how a claim gets denied and your credibility spent.

A found adjustment inside 45 days of the loss cancels it, oldest loss first. And a reimbursement Amazon already paid for that SKU inside 90 days of the loss consumes it, again oldest first. The template's Ledger sheet classifies each row; the Claims sheet is where you enter what survives the netting. On the demo catalogue 5 losses were already settled this way and never become claims.

The window

A warehouse loss becomes eligible 30 days after the adjustment date, because Amazon gives itself that long to find the unit or pay automatically. The window shuts 60 days after the adjustment. That leaves a filing window of a month. Missing it is the single most common way this money is lost.

Value and odds

Units multiplied by landed cost. Approval odds: 85% for a loss, 85% for damage. Amazon's own unreconciled counts approve more often than that; the assumption is deliberately conservative.

How to file

Seller Support, then Fulfillment by Amazon, then FBA issue, then Inventory reimbursement. One case per SKU and event. Paste the ledger row: the date, the reference id, the FNSKU, the quantity, the reason code. State the landed cost and attach the invoice line that proves it. Ask for reimbursement of the units Amazon's own ledger shows as lost or damaged and not yet reimbursed. Log the case id in the Filing log sheet the moment you have it.

Damaged by the carrier, lost in transit

Units that leave your supplier or your warehouse intact and arrive at Amazon damaged, or never arrive, are Amazon's responsibility when they moved on Amazon's partnered carrier. On the demo catalogue this is 11 carrier claims worth $856 and 5 transit claims worth $457.

Where the row is

The same Inventory Ledger, the same Adjustments filter. The codes for carrier damage are H and K; the code for lost in transit is 6. Reason text with the word carrier is carrier damage; reason text with transit and lost is transit loss.

The same netting, the same window

Found units inside 45 days and reimbursements inside 90 days offset the loss, oldest first, exactly as in the warehouse family. The claim opens 30 days after the adjustment and shuts 60 days after it.

Value and odds

Landed cost per unit. Approval odds are lower than for a warehouse loss, 70% for carrier damage and 70% for transit loss, because Amazon more often disputes whether the units were ever in its custody. That is also why the evidence matters more here.

Evidence that wins

The ledger row, as before. Add the shipment id and the carrier tracking that shows the shipment delivered. If you have the box-level contents from your shipping plan, attach the line for the affected units. A claim that shows Amazon's carrier signed for the box and Amazon's own ledger later wrote the units off is hard to deny.

The one this playbook does not cover

An inbound shipment that arrives short, with no adjustment row at all, is a shipment reconciliation claim, not a ledger claim. It lives in a different report with a different window, and it is out of scope here. If your account has a lot of it, that is the next thing to learn.

Refunded, never returned

A customer asks for a refund. Amazon pays it out of your balance. The customer keeps the item. Amazon's policy is that if the return never arrives, you are reimbursed, but the automatic pass misses a share of these, and they are easy to spot yourself. On the demo catalogue it is 24 claims worth $650.

Where the row is

A pair of reports, joined on the order id. In Transactions, filter Type to Refund; each row has the order id, the SKU, the quantity and the amount. In FBA Customer Returns, each return has the same order id. A refund with no return against its order, or with fewer units returned than refunded, is a candidate. The template's Refunds sheet does the join for you: it counts returned units per order and shows the shortfall.

The clock has a floor and a ceiling

A refund younger than 45 days is not a claim yet; the customer still has time to send it back, and filing early gets you a form reply. The claim becomes eligible 60 days after the refund date and shuts 120 days after it. That is a long window, which is exactly why these get forgotten.

Value and odds

Here the value is not landed cost. It is the refunded amount per unit, because that is what left your balance and it is the most Amazon will return. Treat it as a ceiling. Approval odds: 70%.

What disqualifies a candidate

An order Amazon already reimbursed under a return-related reason in the Reimbursements report. A return that arrived and was graded, whatever the grade. A refund on an order that was never shipped by FBA.

How to file

Seller Support, Fulfillment by Amazon, then the customer return option, then refund without return. Give the order id, the SKU, the refund date and the amount. Say that the returns report shows no unit received for the order and that the return window has passed. Log the case.

Returns Amazon damaged

When a return arrives, Amazon grades it. Some grades are your problem and some are Amazon's. On the demo catalogue 26 returns worth $237 were Amazon's.

Where the row is

FBA Customer Returns, the column called detailed disposition. A disposition of CARRIER_DAMAGED or DAMAGED means Amazon or its carrier damaged the unit after the customer sent it back, and that is a claim. A disposition of CUSTOMER_DAMAGED, DEFECTIVE, EXPIRED is customer damage, a defect, or expiry, and none of those is Amazon's liability. Sellable means the unit went back into your inventory and there is nothing to claim.

On the demo catalogue, 48 returns were sellable, 44 were customer damage or defective, and 6 damaged returns had already been paid. The claims are what is left.

Already paid?

Check the status column on the return. If it contains the word reimbursed, Amazon paid. Check the Reimbursements report for the order id with a return or damage reason. Either one removes the candidate.

The window

Eligible at once; there is no waiting period on a graded return. The window shuts 60 days after the return date. These close fast, and the demo catalogue's soonest deadline at the time of the run was a carrier damaged claim with 2 days left.

Value and odds

Landed cost per unit. Approval odds: 60%, the lowest in this playbook, because Amazon will sometimes regrade the return on review. File anyway; the expected value is still positive on almost every row.

How to file

Seller Support, Fulfillment by Amazon, then the customer return option, then a damaged return. Give the order id, the license plate number from the returns report, the SKU, the disposition Amazon itself recorded, and the return date. Ask for reimbursement at cost for a unit Amazon graded as damaged in its custody. Log the case.

Reversals, filing, and the calendar

Reversals

Sometimes Amazon pays a reimbursement and later takes it back. In the Reimbursements report the row has a negative amount, or an original reimbursement type that says reversal. Each one is a case to review by hand: read the original reimbursement, decide whether the reversal was justified, and if it was not, file with both reimbursement ids. Odds are 50% and the window is 60 days from the reversal date. On the demo catalogue there was 1 reversal worth $32.

Cash or stock

Amazon reimburses in replacement inventory as often as in money. Replacement units are a real remedy, but they are not dollars, and your Filing log should record which you received. The Reimbursements sheet's cash share column shows the split for every payment Amazon has made.

The filing wording

Every case is the same shape: what the report says, what the policy says, what you are asking for.

Amazon's own [report name] dated [date] records [quantity] unit(s) of [SKU / FNSKU] as [Amazon's own words: lost, damaged, unreconciled, refunded without return]. No reimbursement appears against this event in the Reimbursements report. Under the FBA lost and damaged inventory reimbursement policy this event is eligible for reimbursement at cost. Landed cost is [amount] per unit; the invoice line is attached. Please reimburse [quantity] unit(s).

Quote Amazon's report back to Amazon. Never argue; cite.

Triage by expected value

The Claims sheet gives every row a value, an approval probability and an expected value. Sort by expected value, descending. Then look at days left. File everything expiring within 14 days first, whatever its size, because a missed window is worth nothing. Then work down the expected value column. On the demo catalogue the open claims are worth $3,573 in expectation, which at this pace comes to $14,291 over a year, on a catalogue of 24 products. Your number will be different; the template will tell you what it is.

The calendar

Once a week, in this order: export the reports, paste them over the old ones, read the helper columns, add new claims to the Claims sheet, file the expiring ones, update the Filing log. It takes an hour the first time and less after that. Warehouse claims open 30 days after the loss and shut 60 days after it; refunds open at 60 days and shut at 120 days; damaged returns shut at 60 days. Put the weekly export on your calendar and the windows take care of themselves.
